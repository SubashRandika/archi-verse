## Learning objectives

After this module, the learner can:

1. Tell a transient failure (a blip that goes away by itself) from a permanent one, and say
   which errors are worth retrying (timeouts, 429, 502, 503, 504) and which are not (400, 401,
   403, 404).
2. Explain how many clients retrying at once can keep a struggling service down (a retry
   storm), and why retrying immediately is the worst choice.
3. Describe exponential backoff (each wait doubles, up to a cap) and jitter (each client waits
   a random part of that), and why backoff needs jitter to work.
4. Put limits on retries: a maximum number of attempts, an overall deadline, a retry budget,
   and a circuit breaker for longer outages.
5. Explain why retrying a request that changes something (a payment) can do it twice, and how
   an idempotency key makes the retry safe.

## The running example

It continues the shop from the earlier modules. Three shoppers press **Pay** at the same
moment:

- **Shopper A** on a laptop, paying for order 1042.
- **Shopper B** and **Shopper C** on their phones, paying for orders 1043 and 1044.

Each app sends a payment to the **Payments service**, which records it in the **Payments DB**:

```http
POST /payments HTTP/1.1
Host: api.shop.example
Content-Type: application/json

{"orderId":1042,"amount":"12400.00","currency":"LKR"}
```

Payments normally handles about **10 requests per second** and answers in about 50 ms. Today a
new version is being deployed, and for a few seconds Payments is restarting and too busy to
answer. The three shoppers stand for everyone paying at that moment (Review 1).

**Illustrative numbers** (Review 3): waits of **1 s, 2 s and 4 s**; at most **3 retries**, so
**4 attempts** in all (Review 6); a **10 s** deadline. Playback compresses time, so a 4 s wait
takes about a second on screen; the badges always show the real values.

## Chapter 1: Things fail for a moment

### Step 1. A blip

**Scene:** Shopper A sends a payment. Payments turns amber ("restarting") and sends back an
error: `503 Service Unavailable`. A moment later Payments is green again. Badge on Shopper A:
"503 · failed".

#### Beginner

> Shopper A presses Pay. The payment helper on the server is busy for a moment, so it says
> "sorry, not now". A second later it is fine again. Nothing is really broken; the timing was
> just unlucky.
>
> **Like in real life:** you phone a shop and the line is busy. That doesn't mean the shop has
> closed. It means everyone called at once, or the person was away for a minute.

#### Advanced

> Many failures in distributed systems are **transient**: they last milliseconds to seconds and
> go away without anyone acting. Typical causes are a deploy restarting instances, a brief
> overload, a dropped connection, a leader election in a database, or a network hiccup. A
> **permanent** failure (a bad request, a missing resource, a bug) gives the same error every
> time.
>
> **Example:** the failed payment.
>
> ```http
> HTTP/1.1 503 Service Unavailable
> Content-Type: application/json
>
> {"error":"service_unavailable","message":"Please try again shortly."}
> ```

**Terms:** Transient failure · Permanent failure · 503 Service Unavailable

---

### Step 2. Just try again

**Scene:** Shopper A waits a moment, then sends the same payment again. Payments is green,
writes to the Payments DB and answers `201 Created`. Badge on Shopper A: "attempt 2 · paid".

#### Beginner

> Shopper A's app waits a tiny moment and asks again. This time the helper says yes, and the
> payment goes through. Shopper A never even noticed the first try failed.
>
> **Like in real life:** the line was busy, so you wait a moment and call again, and this time
> someone answers.

#### Advanced

> A **retry** sends the same request again after a failure. It is worth it only for errors that
> might succeed next time:
>
> - **Retry:** timeouts, dropped connections, `408`, `429 Too Many Requests`, `502`, `503`,
>   `504`. Often `500` too, carefully.
> - **Don't retry:** `400 Bad Request`, `401`, `403`, `404`, `409`, `422`. The same request will
>   fail the same way; retrying only adds load and delay.
>
> Browsers' `fetch` never retries for you. Libraries do: TanStack Query retries a failed query
> 3 times by default, gRPC has a retry policy in its service config, and the AWS SDKs retry
> throttling and transient errors by default.
>
> **Example:** a retry decision.
>
> ```ts
> function isRetryable(error: HttpError): boolean {
>   if (error.kind === "timeout" || error.kind === "network") return true;
>   return [408, 429, 502, 503, 504].includes(error.status);
> }
> ```

**Terms:** Retry · Retryable error

## Chapter 2: Retrying without making it worse

### Step 3. Everyone retries at once

**Scene:** all three shoppers pay while Payments is restarting. All three get a 503 and retry
**immediately**, again and again: a stream of packets from each. Payments turns red; its badge
climbs to "30 req/s · capacity 10". It cannot recover while the retries keep coming.

#### Beginner

> Now everyone's payment fails at the same moment, and every app asks again straight away, and
> again, and again. The helper was only a little busy before. Now it has three times more
> questions than it can answer, so it stays stuck.
>
> **Like in real life:** a lift is full, and everyone waiting keeps pressing the button
> non-stop. The lift doesn't come any faster; the crowd just grows.

#### Advanced

> Retrying immediately turns a short failure into a long one. Every failed request comes back
> as one or more new requests, so the load on the struggling service multiplies just when it
> has the least capacity. This is a **retry storm**. It can leave a system stuck in an
> overloaded state after the original cause has gone: the restart finished, but the backlog of
> retries keeps it failing (a **metastable failure**).
>
> **Example:** immediate retries during the restart.
>
> ```text
> normal traffic                          10 req/s   (capacity 10 req/s)
> each failure retried 2 more times, now   +20 req/s
> load on Payments                        30 req/s   → every request times out → more retries
> ```

**Terms:** Retry storm · Metastable failure · Load amplification

### Step 4. Exponential backoff

**Scene:** the same failure, but each shopper now waits before retrying, and each wait is
twice as long: 1 s, 2 s, 4 s. Badges on the shoppers count up ("attempt 2 · wait 1 s",
"attempt 3 · wait 2 s", "attempt 4 · wait 4 s"). Payments' load falls, and the fourth attempts
succeed. But the three shoppers' retries always arrive **together**; the badge on Payments says
"3 retries at once".

#### Beginner

> This time each app waits before asking again, and every time it fails, it waits twice as
> long: one second, then two, then four. That gives the helper time to get back on its feet.
> But look: all three apps wait exactly the same, so their questions still arrive at the same
> moment.
>
> **Like in real life:** if the line is busy, you wait a minute before calling again, then two
> minutes, then four. You stop jamming the line. But if all your friends use the same rule,
> you all call back at exactly the same moment.

#### Advanced

> **Exponential backoff** makes the wait before retry _n_ grow geometrically: `base × 2^(n−1)`,
> limited by a **cap** so it never gets absurdly long. Fewer retries reach the service while it
> is down, and quick blips still recover quickly. But clients that failed together keep
> retrying together, in synchronised waves.
>
> **Example:** base 1 s, cap 30 s. This is also TanStack Query's default delay:
> `Math.min(1000 * 2 ** attemptIndex, 30000)`.
>
> ```text
> attempt 1   fails (503)
> wait 1 s    attempt 2   fails
> wait 2 s    attempt 3   fails
> wait 4 s    attempt 4   succeeds        total wait 7 s
> (a 5th try would wait 8 s, then 16 s, then 30 s: the cap)
> ```

**Terms:** Exponential backoff · Base delay · Cap

### Step 5. Add jitter

**Scene:** the same three shoppers, but each wait is now a random part of the backoff, so their
retries drift apart and reach Payments one at a time. Payments' badge: "1 at a time". The
offsets are fixed in the animation, so every replay looks the same (Review 4).

#### Beginner

> Now each app adds a little surprise: it waits a random part of its waiting time, not exactly
> the same as the others. So the questions arrive one by one instead of all at once, and the
> helper can answer each of them.
>
> **Like in real life:** instead of everyone calling back at exactly 2 o'clock, each friend
> calls sometime between 2 and 2:10. The line is never jammed.

#### Advanced

> **Jitter** adds randomness to each wait so that clients that failed together spread out.
> Marc Brooker's AWS Architecture Blog post "Exponential Backoff and Jitter" compared the
> variants. **Full jitter** is simple and works well:
> `sleep = random(0, min(cap, base × 2^attempt))`. It cuts both the total work done by the
> server and the time until clients finish, compared with backoff alone. The animation uses
> fixed "random" values so that replays match.
>
> **Example:** the waits before attempt 2 and attempt 3, with full jitter.
>
> ```text
>              before attempt 2       before attempt 3
>              random(0, 1 s)         random(0, 2 s)
> Shopper A    0.4 s                  1.3 s
> Shopper B    0.9 s                  0.5 s
> Shopper C    0.7 s                  1.8 s
> ```
>
> ```ts
> const delay = Math.random() * Math.min(capMs, baseMs * 2 ** attempt);
> ```

**Terms:** Jitter · Full jitter · Thundering herd

### Step 6. Know when to stop

**Scene:** this time the deploy goes wrong and Payments stays red. Shopper A tries 4 times
with backoff, then stops; the badge reads "gave up after 4 tries", and the app shows a clear
message instead of spinning for ever.

#### Beginner

> Sometimes the helper is not coming back soon. Asking for ever would only make you wait for
> ever. So the app tries a few times, then stops and tells you honestly: "Payment didn't go
> through. You weren't charged. Please try again later."
>
> **Like in real life:** after calling three or four times with no answer, you stop and send a
> message instead. You don't spend the whole day redialling.

#### Advanced

> Every retry loop needs limits:
>
> - **Max attempts:** here 4 (1 + 3 retries).
> - **A deadline:** an overall time budget for the whole operation, here 10 s. Each attempt's
>   timeout must fit inside what is left of it.
> - **A retry budget:** retries as a share of all requests (Google's SRE book suggests about
>   10% per client), so a big outage cannot multiply the load.
> - **A circuit breaker:** when most calls are failing, stop sending even first attempts for a
>   while (see the Circuit Breaker module). Retries handle blips; the breaker handles outages.
>
> Then fail clearly: tell the user what happened and whether anything was charged.
>
> **Example:** the give-up path.
>
> ```text
> attempt 1   t = 0 s     503
> attempt 2   t = 1 s     503
> attempt 3   t = 3 s     503
> attempt 4   t = 7 s     503     → stop: max attempts reached (deadline 10 s)
> UI: "Payment didn't go through. You weren't charged. Please try again in a few minutes."
> ```

**Terms:** Max attempts · Deadline · Retry budget · Circuit breaker

## Chapter 3: Is it safe to repeat?

### Step 7. The double charge

**Scene:** Shopper A pays. Payments charges the card and writes it to the Payments DB, but the
answer is lost on the way back: the response packet stops halfway and turns red (Review 5).
Shopper A's badge: "no answer · timed out". The app retries; Payments charges again. Badge on
the Payments DB: "charged twice".

#### Beginner

> Shopper A pays, and the payment goes through, but the "done!" message gets lost on the way
> back. The app doesn't know it worked, so it asks again, and the helper takes the money a
> second time. Oops.
>
> **Like in real life:** you post a letter with money inside, but you never hear back. So you
> send another letter with more money, and later find out both arrived.

#### Advanced

> A timeout tells the client nothing about what happened on the server: the request may never
> have arrived, may have failed, or may have **succeeded with the response lost**. Retrying is
> safe only if doing the operation twice has the same effect as doing it once: if it is
> **idempotent**.
>
> In HTTP, `GET`, `HEAD`, `PUT` and `DELETE` are defined as idempotent; `POST` and `PATCH` are
> not. That is why TanStack Query retries queries by default but not mutations
> (`retry: 0` for `useMutation`).
>
> **Example:** what each side saw.
>
> ```text
> client    POST /payments  {orderId: 1042}  ──▶
> server    charge LKR 12,400 → saved as pay_81f2 → 201 Created  ──╳  (connection reset)
> client    timeout after 5 s → retry: POST /payments  {orderId: 1042}  ──▶
> server    charge LKR 12,400 → saved as pay_9c07 → 201 Created
> result    two charges for one order
> ```

**Terms:** Idempotent · Idempotent methods · At-least-once

### Step 8. Idempotency keys

**Scene:** Shopper A pays again, this time with a key in the request. The response is lost
again and the app retries with the **same key**. Payments looks the key up in the Payments DB,
finds the first result and returns it without charging. Badge on the Payments DB: "charged
once". This step stays introductory (Review 2).

#### Beginner

> Now the app writes a special number on the payment, like a ticket number, and keeps it. If it
> has to ask again, it sends the same number. The helper checks: "I already did ticket 7f3a",
> and just says "done" again, without taking more money.
>
> **Like in real life:** you write a reference number on the letter. If you send a second
> letter with the same number, the shop knows it's the same order and doesn't charge you again.

#### Advanced

> The client makes a unique **idempotency key** once per operation (not per attempt) and sends
> it with every attempt. The server stores the key with the result of the first request; a
> repeat with the same key gets the stored result instead of a second charge. Stripe's API
> works this way, and the IETF `Idempotency-Key` header draft describes it for any HTTP API.
>
> Details that matter (covered in the Idempotency module): keys expire (Stripe keeps them for
> at least 24 hours), the same key with a different body is an error, and two concurrent
> requests with one key need a lock.
>
> **Example:** the retry, with a key.
>
> ```http
> POST /payments HTTP/1.1
> Host: api.shop.example
> Content-Type: application/json
> Idempotency-Key: 7f3a9c2e-4b1d-4e8a-9f60-2d5c1b8e3a47
>
> {"orderId":1042,"amount":"12400.00","currency":"LKR"}
> ```
>
> ```http
> HTTP/1.1 201 Created
> Content-Type: application/json
>
> {"id":"pay_81f2","status":"succeeded"}      (the first result, not a new charge)
> ```

**Terms:** Idempotency key · Exactly-once effect

## Scenarios

### The server says when (branches from step 4)

1. **A 503 with Retry-After.** _Scene:_ the three shoppers pay while Payments is restarting.
   Payments answers each with `503` and `Retry-After: 5`. Badges on the shoppers: "wait 5 s
   (server said)".
   - _Beginner:_ This time the helper says more than "not now". It says "come back in five
     seconds". So the apps don't have to guess.
   - _Advanced:_ `Retry-After` (RFC 9110) tells the client how long to wait, as seconds
     (`Retry-After: 5`) or an HTTP date. Servers send it with `503` and with `429 Too Many
Requests` (see the Rate Limiting concept). A good client honours it as the minimum wait,
     still adds a little jitter, and gives up if it would pass the deadline.

     ```http
     HTTP/1.1 503 Service Unavailable
     Retry-After: 5
     ```

2. **Everyone comes back at the right time.** _Scene:_ after the wait (with a little jitter),
   the three retries reach Payments, which is ready; all three succeed ("paid").
   - _Beginner:_ The apps waited exactly as long as they were told, and the helper was ready.
   - _Advanced:_ The server knows its own state (a restart takes about 5 s, a rate-limit
     window resets in 5 s), so its hint beats any guess. Without the header, the client falls
     back to its own backoff.

### Retries multiply (branches from step 6)

1. **Two layers retrying.** _Scene:_ the Payments DB turns red. Shopper A's request reaches
   Payments, which retries the DB 4 times before failing; then Shopper A retries, and each of
   its attempts triggers 4 more DB tries. The badge on the DB counts up to "16 queries · 1
   tap".
   - _Beginner:_ The app asks the helper again and again, and each time the helper asks the
     database again and again. One press of a button turns into sixteen questions to the
     database, which is already struggling.
   - _Advanced:_ Retries at several layers multiply: 4 attempts at the client × 4 attempts at
     Payments = up to 16 database calls for one user action. Three layers would make 64. The
     layers also wait for each other, so the user waits far longer than any single deadline.

     ```text
     client attempt 1 → Payments → DB ×4 → 503
     client attempt 2 → Payments → DB ×4 → 503
     client attempt 3 → Payments → DB ×4 → 503
     client attempt 4 → Payments → DB ×4 → 503      = 16 DB calls
     ```

2. **Retry at one layer.** _Scene:_ Payments now fails fast and passes the error up; only
   Shopper A retries. The DB badge: "4 queries · 1 tap".
   - _Beginner:_ Now only the app asks again. The helper just says "the database isn't
     answering" straight away. Four questions instead of sixteen.
   - _Advanced:_ Retry at a single layer, usually the one that knows whether the operation is
     safe to repeat and has the user's deadline. Inner layers fail fast and return a retryable
     error (with `Retry-After` if they know). Retry budgets and circuit breakers cap the rest.

## Components

### Shopper A, Shopper B, Shopper C (client, mobile)

- **Purpose:** _Beginner:_ people paying in the shop's web site and app. _Advanced:_ the
  clients that own the retry policy: which errors to retry, backoff with jitter, max attempts,
  deadline, and an idempotency key per payment.
- **Talks to:** Payments.
- **Technologies:** `fetch` with a small retry helper, TanStack Query (`retry`, `retryDelay`),
  ky, axios-retry, OkHttp and URLSession interceptors on mobile.
- **Advantages / limitations:** retries hide blips from users / they add load and delay, and
  are unsafe for operations that aren't idempotent.

### Payments (service)

- **Purpose:** _Beginner:_ the helper that takes the money. _Advanced:_ a service that charges
  cards and records payments. During a deploy it briefly returns `503`; it should send
  `Retry-After` when it knows, and it stores idempotency keys with their results.
- **Responsibilities:** charge once per idempotency key · return clear, retryable errors · fail
  fast instead of retrying its own dependencies at every layer.
- **Talks to:** the shoppers' apps, Payments DB.

### Payments DB (database)

- **Purpose:** _Beginner:_ the notebook where every payment is written down. _Advanced:_ stores
  charges and an idempotency table (key, request fingerprint, stored response, expiry), with a
  unique constraint on the key.

## Review

1. **Three shoppers stand for everyone.** The scene has a laptop and two phones; the badges use
   service-level numbers ("30 req/s · capacity 10"), and the text says the three stand for all
   the people paying at that moment. _Recommended:_ three is enough to show retries arriving
   together and drifting apart, and stays readable.
2. **Idempotency stays introductory in steps 7–8.** The double charge and the key are shown;
   expiry, mismatched bodies and concurrent requests get one sentence and a pointer to the
   Idempotency concept. _Recommended._
3. **Illustrative numbers and compressed time:** 10 req/s capacity, waits of 1/2/4 s, a 10 s
   deadline, 5 s timeout. Playback compresses time; badges show the real values.
   _Recommended._
4. **Fixed jitter offsets.** Step 5's "random" waits are fixed values (the table above), so
   every replay looks the same and the e2e tests are stable; the Advanced text says real
   clients use `Math.random()`. _Recommended._
5. **How the lost response is drawn (step 7):** the response packet stops halfway on its wire
   and turns red (the existing `stay` option with the error flow), clicking it shows
   "connection reset", and the shopper's badge reads "no answer · timed out". No engine change.
   _Recommended._ The alternative is to skip the response and only show the timeout badge,
   which hides the key fact that the server **did** succeed.
6. **"3 attempts" read as 3 retries (4 attempts in all).** The approved plan said "3 attempts"
   and waits of 1, 2 and 4 s; three waits need four attempts. With 4 attempts per layer,
   "Retries multiply" becomes 4 × 4 = **16** queries (not 9). _Recommended:_ keep 4 attempts,
   since three doubling waits show the pattern best. The alternative is 3 attempts in all
   (waits 1 s and 2 s only) and 3 × 3 = 9.
