---

## Learning objectives

After this module, the learner can:

1. Explain how one slow service can bring down the services that call it (a cascading
   failure), and why timeouts alone do not stop it.
2. Describe what a circuit breaker does, and its three states: closed, open and half-open.
3. Explain what trips a breaker (failures and slow calls counted over a window) and what lets
   it close again (a trial call after a cooling-off period).
4. Say why failing fast helps both sides: the caller stays responsive and the failing service
   gets room to recover.
5. Choose a sensible fallback (cached data, a default, hiding a feature) and know where
   breakers live in practice (in code, in a gateway, in a service mesh).

## The running example

It continues the API Gateway story. The learner opens "My orders" again. The gateway routes the
request to the **Orders service**, which needs the payment status of each order, so it calls
the **Payments service**:

```http
GET /payments?orderIds=1042,1043 HTTP/1.1
Host: payments.internal
traceparent: 00-4bf92f3577b34da6a3ce929d0e0e4736-00f067aa0ba902b7-01
```

Normally Payments answers in about 40 ms. Today its database is overloaded, so Payments takes
10 seconds or more to answer, or doesn't answer at all.

Inside the Orders service, every call to Payments goes through a **circuit breaker** with these
settings (the numbers the scene shows):

| Setting                    | Value                            |
| -------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Call timeout               | 2 s (a slower call is a failure) |
| Trip when                  | 5 failures in a row              |
| Stay open for              | 30 s                             |
| Trial calls when half-open | 1                                |

| Component        | Kind          | Role            | Notes                                                              |
| ---------------- | ------------- | --------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Browser          | client        | Client          | The shop's page, as in API Gateway                                 |
| Orders service   | service       | Backend service | The caller. Badges show how many of its 50 threads are busy        |
| Circuit breaker  | breaker (new) | Resilience      | On the wire from Orders to Payments; part of Orders' code (Review) |
| Payments service | service       | Backend service | The dependency that slows down                                     |
| Payments DB      | database      | Data store      | The cause: overloaded, "slow"                                      |

Connections: browser–Orders, Orders–breaker, breaker–Payments, Payments–Payments DB. The API
gateway is left out to keep the picture small; step 1 says it is still there.

The **breaker** is a new shape: a small switch box with three lamps on top (closed, half-open,
open). Its state shows as a tint and a badge ("closed", "open · 30 s", "half-open"), and the
lamp of the new state flashes when it switches (**Review 3**). A failure counter badge shows
"3 of 5 failures".

Colours as before: requests blue, responses green, errors rose, warnings (slow, half-open)
amber.

## Chapter 1: When a service gets slow

### Step 1. A healthy chain

**Scene:** a request travels browser → Orders → breaker → Payments → Payments DB, and the
answers come back. The breaker is "closed" (calls pass straight through). Everything is quick.

#### Beginner

> When you open "My orders", the Orders service makes the list. But it doesn't know if each
> order is paid, so it asks the Payments service. Payments looks in its notebook (the
> database) and answers. All of this takes less time than a blink.
>
> **Like in real life:** a waiter takes your order, and asks the kitchen if the soup is ready.
> The kitchen answers straight away, and the waiter tells you.

#### Advanced

> Orders calls Payments synchronously while it builds the response: one request, one HTTP call
> downstream, and a database query behind that. In a healthy system the whole chain takes
> about 60 ms, and Orders' thread is busy for all of it. The breaker sits around the call in
> Orders' code and is **closed**: it only watches, recording each call's result and time.
>
> **Example:** the call and its answer.
>
> ```http
> GET /payments?orderIds=1042,1043 HTTP/1.1      →  200 OK in 38 ms
> [{"orderId":1042,"status":"paid"},{"orderId":1043,"status":"pending"}]
> ```

**Terms:** Dependency · Synchronous call · Circuit breaker

---

### Step 2. One slow service, everyone waits

**Scene:** Payments DB turns amber ("slow"). Several requests arrive at Orders and go on to
Payments, but no answers come back. The Orders badge counts up: "12 of 50 threads busy", then
"38 of 50". The browser waits ("waiting").

#### Beginner

> Today the Payments notebook is very slow. Payments asks it, and waits, and waits. Orders
> asks Payments, and waits too. More people open "My orders", so more and more of Orders'
> helpers are stuck waiting. Soon Orders has no free helpers left, even for people who
> don't need Payments at all.
>
> **Like in real life:** the kitchen stops answering. Every waiter who asks stands at the
> kitchen door, waiting. After a while, no waiter is left to take new orders, and the whole
> restaurant stops, even though only the kitchen has a problem.

#### Advanced

> This is a **cascading failure**. Each call to Payments holds one of Orders' threads (or
> connections, or event-loop slots) until it returns. When Payments slows from 40 ms to 10 s,
> the number of calls in flight grows by the same factor (Little's law: in flight = arrival
> rate × time). At 20 requests per second and 10 s per call, Orders would need 200 threads;
> it has 50. Requests that never touch Payments start failing too.
>
> A 2 s **timeout** helps: each stuck call now holds a thread for 2 s, not 10. But Orders
> still pays 2 s for every call it already knows will fail, and keeps sending load to a
> service that is struggling.
>
> **Example:** 20 req/s × 2 s timeout = 40 threads busy just waiting for Payments, out of 50.

**Terms:** Cascading failure · Thread pool · Timeout · Little's law

## Chapter 2: The breaker

### Step 3. Meet the circuit breaker

**Scene:** focus on the breaker. Its "closed" lamp flashes; badge "closed · 0 of 5 failures".
One call passes through it.

#### Beginner

> The circuit breaker is a small guard that stands between Orders and Payments. It watches
> every call. As long as Payments answers well, it lets everything through. That's called
> **closed**, like a closed door in a pipe: the water flows.
>
> **Like in real life:** the fuse box in your house. Normally the electricity flows. If
> something goes wrong too many times, it switches off to keep the house safe.

#### Advanced

> A circuit breaker wraps calls to one dependency and keeps a small state machine:
> **closed** (calls pass, results are counted), **open** (calls fail at once, without trying)
> and **half-open** (a few trial calls test whether the dependency has recovered). It lives
> in the caller, usually as a library around the HTTP client, one breaker per dependency (or
> per endpoint).
>
> **Example:** with resilience4j (Java) the settings above look like this.
>
> ```text
> slidingWindowType=COUNT_BASED   slidingWindowSize=5   minimumNumberOfCalls=5
> failureRateThreshold=100        slowCallRateThreshold=100   slowCallDurationThreshold=2s
> waitDurationInOpenState=30s     permittedNumberOfCallsInHalfOpenState=1
> ```
>
> A window of the last 5 calls that must all fail is "5 failures in a row". Slow calls count
> through their own rate; the 2 s timeout itself is set on the HTTP client (or a TimeLimiter).

**Terms:** Closed state · Open state · Half-open state

---

### Step 4. Failures add up

**Scene:** five calls go from Orders through the breaker to Payments. Each one times out
(after 2 s, compressed) and comes back as a rose error. The breaker's badge counts "1 of 5
failures" … "5 of 5 failures".

#### Beginner

> Payments is still slow. Each time Orders asks, it gives up after two seconds and comes back
> with "no answer". The guard keeps count: one failure, two, three, four, five.
>
> **Like in real life:** you call a friend and nobody picks up. You try again. And again.
> After five tries you stop and think: "they're busy, I'll call later."

#### Advanced

> The breaker records each outcome. A call fails if it throws, returns an error the policy
> counts (5xx, connection refused) or takes longer than the slow-call threshold. 4xx answers
> usually do **not** count: they are the caller's mistake, not a sick dependency.
>
> Real breakers rarely use "5 in a row". They use a **failure rate over a sliding window**
> (for example 50 % of the last 20 calls, once at least 10 calls were made), so one unlucky
> call in a busy minute doesn't trip it. The scene uses 5 in a row to keep the counting
> visible (**Review 4**).
>
> **Example:** a count-based window of the last 10 calls.
>
> ```text
> ✓ ✓ ✗ ✓ ✗ ✗ ✓ ✗ ✗ ✗   →  6 of 10 failed = 60 %  ≥ 50 %  →  trip
> ```

**Terms:** Failure threshold · Sliding window · Slow call

---

### Step 5. The breaker trips

**Scene:** the breaker's "open" lamp flashes, its tint turns rose, badge "open · 30 s". The
breaker–Payments wire dims.

#### Beginner

> Five failures! The guard decides: "Payments is not well. Let's stop asking for a while."
> It **opens**: no more calls go to Payments for 30 seconds.
>
> **Like in real life:** the fuse switches off. The lights go out in one room, but the rest of
> the house is safe, and nothing burns.

#### Advanced

> The failure threshold was reached, so the breaker moves from closed to **open** and starts
> its wait timer (30 s here). The transition is an event worth logging and alerting on: it is
> the clearest signal that a dependency is unhealthy.
>
> **Example:** the log line and the metric.
>
> ```text
> 12:04:17.302 WARN CircuitBreaker 'payments' changed state from CLOSED to OPEN
>              (failure rate 100.0 %, 5 of 5 calls)
> resilience4j_circuitbreaker_state{name="payments",state="open"} 1
> ```

**Terms:** Trip · Wait duration

### Step 6. Failing fast, with a fallback

**Scene:** new requests reach Orders and go to the breaker, which sends each one straight
back (a short rose packet; no packet reaches Payments). Orders answers the browser at once
with a green response: the orders, with payment status "unknown". The Orders badge drops
to "3 of 50 threads busy"; Payments' tint calms down.

#### Beginner

> Now when Orders asks, the guard answers right away: "Payments is resting, don't wait." So
> Orders doesn't get stuck. It shows your orders anyway, and says "payment status: we'll
> check again soon". And Payments gets some quiet time to get better.
>
> **Like in real life:** the waiter knows the kitchen is in trouble, so he doesn't go and
> wait at the door. He tells you: "the soup will take a while, can I bring you bread now?"

#### Advanced

> While open, the breaker rejects calls immediately (`CallNotPermittedException`), in
> microseconds instead of a 2 s timeout. That frees Orders' threads, keeps its latency low and
> removes load from Payments, which helps it recover.
>
> A **fallback** turns the fast failure into a useful answer. Options, best first: cached or
> last-known data, a safe default ("unknown"), a reduced feature (hide the payment column),
> or an honest error. The fallback must be cheap and must not call the failing dependency
> again.
>
> **Example:** Orders' answer during the outage.
>
> ```http
> HTTP/1.1 200 OK
> Content-Type: application/json
>
> {"orders":[{"id":1042,"paymentStatus":"unknown"},{"id":1043,"paymentStatus":"unknown"}],
>  "degraded":["payments"]}
> ```

**Terms:** Fail fast · Fallback · Graceful degradation

## Chapter 3: Recovery

### Step 7. Half-open: one careful try

**Scene:** the breaker's badge counts down "open · 20 s", "10 s", "0 s"; its "half-open"
lamp flashes and the tint turns amber ("half-open"). Exactly one call goes through to
Payments, which answers quickly (green). Other requests meanwhile still fail fast.

#### Beginner

> After 30 seconds the guard thinks: "Maybe Payments feels better now. Let's try once." It
> lets **one** call through, and only one. Everyone else still gets the quick answer. This is
> **half-open**: the door is open just a crack.
>
> **Like in real life:** after a fuse trips, you switch it back on carefully, and watch. You
> don't plug everything in at once.

#### Advanced

> When the wait duration ends, the breaker moves to **half-open** and permits a small number
> of trial calls (1 here, often 3–10). Their results decide: if they succeed, the breaker
> closes; if one fails, it opens again with a fresh timer. Limiting trials matters: a
> recovering service that suddenly gets full traffic often falls over again.
>
> **Example:** the timeline.
>
> ```text
> 12:04:17  CLOSED → OPEN       5 of 5 failed
> 12:04:47  OPEN → HALF_OPEN    wait of 30 s over, 1 trial call permitted
> 12:04:47  trial call          200 OK in 45 ms
> ```

**Terms:** Trial call · Probe

---

### Step 8. Closed again

**Scene:** the "closed" lamp flashes, the tint clears, badge "closed · 0 of 5 failures".
Normal traffic flows again through the whole chain, as in step 1. Then all three lamps flash
in order: closed → open → half-open → closed (the full cycle).

#### Beginner

> The try worked! Payments is better. The guard **closes** again, and everything flows like
> before. It starts counting from zero, and keeps watching.
>
> The guard has three moods: **closed** (all good, let calls through), **open** (something is
> wrong, stop calling for a while) and **half-open** (try once, carefully).
>
> **Like in real life:** the room's lights are back on. The fuse box is still there, ready to
> switch off again if something goes wrong.

#### Advanced

> A successful trial closes the breaker and resets its window. The whole cycle protects both
> sides automatically: no operator had to notice the outage, switch Payments off or on, or
> redeploy Orders.
>
> Breakers work best with their neighbours: **timeouts** turn hangs into failures it can
> count, **bulkheads** (a separate thread or connection pool per dependency) stop one
> dependency from using up all of Orders' capacity, **retries with backoff** handle short
> blips but must stop when the breaker is open, and good **fallbacks** decide what the user
> sees.
>
> **Example:** where the breaker can live.
>
> | Where                | Example                                          |
> | -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
> | In the caller's code | resilience4j, Polly (.NET), opossum (Node)       |
> | In the API gateway   | Spring Cloud Gateway, Kong passive health checks |
> | In a service mesh    | Envoy outlier detection, Istio                   |

**Terms:** Bulkhead · Retry with backoff · Service mesh

## Scenarios

### Without a breaker: the failure spreads (branches from step 2) (**Review 1**)

1. **The queue grows.** _Scene:_ calls keep going to the slow Payments; Orders' badge climbs
   "38 of 50", "50 of 50 threads busy" and turns rose.
   - _Beginner:_ There is no guard. Orders keeps asking Payments, and keeps waiting. All of its
     helpers are stuck now.
   - _Advanced:_ Thread-pool exhaustion: with no breaker and no bulkhead, the slow dependency
     takes every thread. Queues fill next, then memory.
2. **Even unrelated pages fail.** _Scene:_ the browser asks Orders for something that doesn't
   need Payments ("My profile"); Orders can't take it and answers 503 (rose).
   - _Beginner:_ Now even "My profile", which never needs Payments, doesn't work. One sick
     service made a healthy one sick too.
   - _Advanced:_ The failure has crossed a service boundary. Health checks start failing, the
     load balancer pulls Orders instances out, and the remaining ones get even more load.
3. **Retries make it worse.** _Scene:_ the browser retries several times (a burst); every
   retry also gets stuck or fails.
   - _Beginner:_ People press "reload" again and again. Each press adds more waiting.
   - _Advanced:_ A **retry storm**: retries multiply load on an already failing system. Retry
     budgets, exponential backoff with jitter, and a breaker that stops retrying are the cure.

### The trial call fails (branches from step 7)

1. **Payments is still sick.** _Scene:_ the one trial call times out (rose); the breaker's
   badge shows "half-open · failed".
   - _Beginner:_ The careful try didn't work. Payments still can't answer.
   - _Advanced:_ One failed trial is enough to reopen: the breaker doesn't wait for a full
     window, because it already knows the dependency was unhealthy.
2. **Open again.** _Scene:_ "open" lamp flashes, badge "open · 30 s"; requests fail fast
   again, with the fallback.
   - _Beginner:_ The guard closes the crack and waits another 30 seconds before trying again.
     Your orders still show, with "payment status: unknown".
   - _Advanced:_ The timer restarts. Some libraries grow the wait each time (exponential, with
     a cap) so a long outage gets fewer probes. Persistent opening is an alert, not just a log
     line.

## Components

### Browser (client)

As in API Gateway, briefly. Talks to: Orders service.

### Orders service

- **Purpose:** _Beginner:_ makes your list of orders. _Advanced:_ the caller; builds the
  orders response and calls Payments for each order's payment status.
- **Responsibilities:** build the response · call Payments through the breaker · use a fallback
  when the breaker is open · protect its own threads.
- **How it works:** a pool of 50 worker threads; each request holds one until it answers.
- **Talks to:** browser (through the gateway), Payments (through the breaker).
- **Technologies:** Spring Boot, ASP.NET Core, Node.js. **Limitations:** its capacity is shared
  by every request, so one slow dependency can use it all up without a breaker or bulkhead.

### Circuit breaker

- **Purpose:** _Beginner:_ a guard that stops Orders from calling Payments when Payments is
  unwell, and tries again later. _Advanced:_ a state machine around calls to one dependency
  that turns repeated failures into immediate rejections, then probes for recovery.
- **Responsibilities:** count outcomes · trip on too many failures or slow calls · fail fast
  while open · allow trial calls when half-open · report state changes.
- **Inputs / outputs:** calls and their outcomes / calls passed on, or rejected at once.
- **Technologies:** resilience4j, Polly, opossum; in proxies, Envoy outlier detection and
  Istio.
- **Advantages / limitations:** protects the caller and gives the dependency time to recover
  / needs tuning (thresholds, window, wait), and is only useful with a good fallback.

### Payments service

- **Purpose:** _Beginner:_ knows whether each order is paid. _Advanced:_ the dependency; owns
  payment state, answers payment-status queries.
- **Limitations:** depends on its own database; when that is overloaded, Payments slows down.

### Payments DB

- **Purpose:** _Beginner:_ Payments' notebook. _Advanced:_ the payments database, overloaded
  in this story (for example by a long-running report).

## Review

1. **Second scenario:** "Without a breaker: the failure spreads" (above), instead of
   "Graceful degradation", which the plan named. Step 6 already teaches fallbacks, and this
   scenario shows what the breaker prevents. _Recommended._
2. **The breaker as its own box** on the Orders → Payments wire, although it is a library
   inside Orders. It makes the state visible; step 3 says where it really lives.
   _Recommended._ The alternative is to show it on the Orders rack, which is harder to read.
3. **How the state shows:** a tint and a badge ("closed", "open · 30 s", "half-open"), with
   the new state's lamp flashing as it switches. No engine change. A lamp that **stays** lit
   would need a small engine addition (a lasting stage state). _Recommended: flashing lamps
   now; add lasting lamps only if they turn out to be missed._
4. **"5 failures in a row"** in the scene, with rate-based sliding windows (the usual
   production setting) explained in Advanced (step 4). _Recommended_, to keep the counting
   visible.
5. **Compressed time:** the 2 s timeouts and the 30 s wait play in about a second each, with
   badges showing the real values. _Recommended._
